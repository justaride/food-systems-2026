# The real feed loss from Norwegian salmon farming

## Main finding

The slogan that “70% of the feed in Norwegian salmon farming ends up in the fjord” is **misleading, but not entirely baseless**. If the phrase is used as a crude **whole-feed mass balance**, it is in the right order of magnitude: the best feed-resource accounting from entity["organization","Nofima","norwegian food research"] found that only about **33% of feed dry matter** was retained in the whole salmon in 2020, which implies that about **67% was not retained in fish biomass**. A coastal mass-balance study of commercial open-net pen farming, meanwhile, found that about **39–43%** of feed-derived C/N/P was retained in salmon, while **57–61%** was released to the surrounding environment; within that released fraction, about **18%** was particulate organic waste and **39–43%** was dissolved inorganic waste. So, as a **total non-retained feed-derived matter** claim, “around two-thirds” is broadly defensible. As a claim about **uneaten pellets** or “feed just sinking to the fjord floor”, it is wrong. citeturn6view0turn12view0turn11view2

The most important correction is therefore this: in open-net pens, the dominant loss pathway is **not uneaten feed**, but **metabolised and excreted material**. Public Norwegian literature reviewed here does **not** provide a robust national farm-by-farm mean for pellet spill, but it does consistently show that most of the non-retained feed ends up as **dissolved nitrogen and phosphorus** plus **faecal solids**, not as intact pellets. citeturn12view0turn11view2turn21view0

## Open-net pens

For open-net pens in entity["country","Norway","scandinavia"], the most defensible answer to “how much feed dry matter is lost to the environment?” is **roughly 60–70%**, depending on which accounting frame is used. On a direct feed-resource basis, Nofima’s 2022 report on 2020 production found **33% dry-matter retention** in whole salmon. On a nutrient-flow basis, Wang and Olsen’s 2023 coastal analysis found **57–61% environmental release** of feed-derived nutrients, with the **northern region** performing better than the **southern region** because of better feed utilisation. That gives a useful operational variation range of about **4 percentage points** at regional scale, but the public literature does not provide a transparent national distribution of this metric farm by farm. citeturn6view0turn12view0turn11view2

That distinction matters. The Nofima result answers the broad resource-efficiency question: how much of the feed ends up in the fish at harvest? The Wang and Olsen result answers the environmental-flow question: how much feed-derived material is discharged from the farm system? Both point in the same direction: **only about one-third to two-fifths is retained**, and **most of the rest leaves the farm one way or another**. citeturn6view0turn12view0

## Where the feed goes

The best public Norwegian split is **not** “pellets versus no pellets”, but **particulate versus dissolved**. In Wang and Olsen’s commercial-farm analysis, about **18%** of total feed-derived C/N/P was discharged as **organic waste**, and **39–43%** as **inorganic nutrient waste**. They also showed that the **majority of phosphorus** was released as **organic waste**, whereas **carbon and nitrogen** were mainly released in **inorganic form**, especially as carbon dioxide and ammonia. citeturn12view0turn11view2

For the particulate fraction, the same study’s regional release rates indicate about **6.2 kg particulate nitrogen per tonne of feed** and about **6.7 kg particulate phosphorus per tonne of feed**, with little regional variation in the particulate component. This is the fraction that mostly reflects **faeces plus any uneaten feed** and that primarily affects the seabed beneath and around cages. citeturn12view1

For the dissolved fraction, the regional release rates were about **25.9–28.1 kg dissolved inorganic nitrogen per tonne of feed** and **2.4–2.6 kg dissolved inorganic phosphorus per tonne of feed**. entity["organization","Havforskningsinstituttet","norwegian marine research"] also summarised several Norwegian models that put dissolved emissions at **20–38.4 kg DIN per tonne fish** and **1.5–5.1 kg DIP per tonne fish**, depending on method. This is the large, mostly invisible part of the waste budget and is the main reason the popular claim is misleading when it is framed as pellet spill. citeturn12view1turn10search12

The weakest part of the public evidence is the exact national split between **uneaten feed** and **faeces** in open-net grow-out. The sources reviewed here do not publish a robust nationwide pellet-loss mean. What they do show is that **feed spill is operationally important but uncertain**: the 2024 SINTEF memo notes that when primary data are unavailable, scenario work often assumes **10% feed loss**, while industry indications suggest it can be **as much as 15%** in worse cases; the same memo explicitly states that sludge consists of **feed loss and faeces**. entity["company","AkvaFuture","norwegian salmon company"]’s final report reinforces how sensitive sludge is to feed spill, noting that even small changes in pellet loss change sludge amount and composition enough to matter for internal control. In other words: **pellet loss is real, but it is not the same thing as total feed loss to the environment**. citeturn21view0turn19view0

## Closed and semi-closed systems

Closed and semi-closed systems mainly change the **solid** part of the waste budget. The strongest general statement in the public Norwegian material is from the 2024 SINTEF memo: in traditional open-net farms, sludge is emitted directly to the water; in land-based and closed technologies, solids can be collected on-site; a review of closed and semi-closed systems reported **60–70% sludge collection efficiency**, a 2021 policy proposal used **60%** as a threshold, but the memo argues that real current performance may be lower and therefore models **50% sludge collection** for closed sea-based technology. The same memo also states that **dissolved nutrients are assumed not to be collected**. That last point is decisive: even very good sludge capture does **not** eliminate most dissolved N and P losses. citeturn21view0turn42view3

AkvaFuture’s 2020 final report gives a concrete operational number: at Hamnsundet in 2019, the company used **3,530.8 tonnes of feed** and **collected 75 tonnes of dry sludge**, equal to about **2.1% of feed mass**. The dry sludge contained **7.3% nitrogen** and **2.4% phosphorus** on a dry-matter basis. That is a real, field-based recovery number, but it is clearly **not** equivalent to “total waste prevented”, because dissolved losses remain and the report itself stresses that feed spill and sludge composition remained major operational issues. citeturn18view0turn19view3turn19view0

For FishGLOBE, the public documentation is more qualitative than quantitative. The CtrlAQUA/Nofima fact sheet describes **high collection of sludge**, **low feed waste**, **fast removal of particles**, and by late 2024 the company reported **around 4.5 million post-smolts produced** with sludge collection, no lice treatment and no escapes. However, I did **not** locate an independent public capture-efficiency percentage comparable to the Mowi/NIVA study. So FishGLOBE clearly reduces feed loss pathways relative to open pens, but the exact public, citable percentage remains weakly documented. citeturn36view1turn36view0

Ocean Farm 1 is different again, because it is **not a closed solids-capture concept**. Public descriptions of the unit characterise it as a **semi-offshore/open-net pen** facility. That means it may change **location, exposure and dilution**, but it does **not** fundamentally change the source pathways of feed-derived particulate and dissolved waste in the way that closed or land-based systems do. So Ocean Farm 1 should **not** be counted as a technology that materially reduces feed loss at source; it is better understood as a different kind of **open-cage** production. citeturn39search13turn39search14turn42view3

Andfjord Salmon’s system is closer to a land-based answer to the waste problem. Its 2024 report says the company uses a specially designed feed with **lower sink rate** so that more is eaten and less ends up as waste; ROVs then clean **feed residues and faeces** from the pool floor and pump them to water treatment. The same report also states that the first production cycle delivered **97.5% survival** and **FCR 1.05**. But it also states explicitly that **no salmon were farmed during 2024**, because the year was devoted to build-out at Kvalnes. So the public 2024 material shows a clear **solids-capture design**, but it does not provide a new production-cycle recovery percentage for that year. citeturn41view0turn41view1turn40view0

For Mowi, the best documented public sea-based closed-case I found is **not Steigen**, but a peer-reviewed/NIVA article on a Mowi Ecomerd site in Matersfjorden. There, **613 tonnes of feed** produced **528 tonnes of biomass** at **FCR 1.16**. The system recaptured **20.52 tonnes of dry matter**, which is about **33 g dry matter per kg feed added**. The estimated recapture efficiency was only about **11% of solids**, **3% of nitrogen**, **9% of phosphorus** and **8% of total organic carbon**. The authors explain the modest result largely through the system design: **99% of water bypassed the separator**, so most dissolved and finely diluted waste still exited to the recipient. That is an important reality check for all floating closed systems. citeturn30view0turn31search0turn42view1

## What FCR and bFCR actually tell you

The cleanest public definition in the Norwegian material comes from the 2024 SINTEF memo. It defines **economic FCR** as feed given divided by biomass produced, and states that it **includes feed loss and feed given to fish that die during production**. It contrasts this with **biological FCR**, which refers to the amount **eaten**. In practical terms, the gap between FCR and bFCR is the farm’s **operational loss margin**: spillage, mortality-related feed use, and stock-accounting effects. citeturn21view0

What that gap does **not** tell you is the full environmental waste budget. Even when bFCR is good, a large share of eaten feed still leaves the farm as dissolved excretion and faeces. That is exactly why Nofima can find only **33% dry-matter retention** in harvested fish while Wang and Olsen still find **57–61% of feed-derived material discharged**. So the difference **FCR minus bFCR** is a useful indicator of **avoidable operational loss**, but it is a poor proxy for **total feed-derived emissions**. A small FCR–bFCR gap can coexist with a large nutrient discharge. citeturn6view0turn12view0turn21view0

The practical implication is straightforward. If someone uses a company’s low FCR to argue that little feed is lost to the environment, they are conflating **feeding efficiency** with **nutrient retention efficiency**. They are related, but they are not the same metric. citeturn21view0turn6view0turn12view0

## National nitrogen and phosphorus loads

A single audited official national inventory of **total** N and P emissions from Norwegian salmon farming for each of **2022–2024** is not published in one easily citable place in the public material reviewed here. The most defensible way to quantify the national scale is therefore as a **mass-balance estimate**, not as a claimed official emissions ledger. The production side is official: Norway sold about **1.65 million tonnes** of farmed fish in **2022**, and **1.665 million tonnes** in **2024**; for **2024**, the Directorate of Fisheries reports **1.553 million tonnes of salmon**, **95,000 tonnes of rainbow trout**, and says salmon volume was **almost unchanged from 2023 to 2024**, so 2023 was essentially the same scale. citeturn45search8turn46view0

Using the public gross-discharge coefficients in the NIVA/Mowi mass balance — about **56.5 kg N** and **11.7 kg P per tonne biomass** before the very modest separator recovery in that system — the national order of magnitude comes out at roughly **93–94 thousand tonnes of nitrogen per year** and about **19–19.5 thousand tonnes of phosphorus per year** at 2022–2024 production levels. Because 2024 was almost flat against 2023, and 2022 was only slightly lower in total sold volume, the annual totals for the three years are best read as **roughly stable**, not as sharply different from year to year. This is an inference from public mass-balance coefficients and official production statistics, not an official audited national inventory. citeturn42view1turn46view0turn45search8

Against land-based agriculture, the best simple national comparator I found from entity["organization","Statistisk sentralbyra","norwegian statistics office"] is **total nitrogen and phosphorus available to agriculture from mineral fertiliser plus livestock manure**. Those totals were **149,000 tonnes N and 20,400 tonnes P in 2022**, **136,900 tonnes N and 18,400 tonnes P in 2023**, and **140,000 tonnes N and 19,000 tonnes P in 2024**. On that comparator, aquaculture appears to be **smaller than total agriculture for nitrogen**, but of a **similar national order of magnitude for phosphorus**. Because the SSB comparator includes both mineral fertiliser and manure, it is **not** a clean pure-livestock benchmark; public one-line national totals for livestock manure alone were not located in the reviewed sources. citeturn43search9turn43search6turn43search0

That broad national picture is consistent with local coastal evidence. In the Hardangerfjord example highlighted by Havforskningsinstituttet, average annual discharges to sea in 2013–2023 were **375 tonnes N from aquaculture versus 506 tonnes N from agriculture**, but **65 tonnes P from aquaculture versus 9.3 tonnes P from agriculture**. In other words, aquaculture phosphorus can dominate strongly in some fjord systems even where nitrogen does not. citeturn10search10

## Assessment of the 70 percent claim

The most accurate verdict is this. If the claim is intended to mean that **most feed dry matter is not retained in harvested fish**, it is **roughly right in scale**: the best Norwegian evidence points to **about 60–70% not retained**, depending on whether you use whole-fish dry-matter accounting or nutrient-flow accounting. If the claim is intended to mean that **70% of feed is uneaten pellets ending up on the seabed**, it is **wrong**. The evidence reviewed here shows that the dominant losses are **dissolved metabolic waste** and **faeces**, with uneaten feed a smaller, operationally important but much less well documented share. citeturn6view0turn12view0turn21view0

So the real quantitative answer is not “70% of the feed becomes pellet waste in the fjord”. It is closer to this: **about one-third of feed dry matter is retained in fish; about two-thirds is lost from the farming system; and most of that lost fraction leaves as dissolved excretion and faecal material, not as intact feed pellets**. Closed and semi-closed systems can reduce the particulate part, sometimes substantially, but current public evidence shows that **dissolved N and P remain hard to capture**, and even some floating closed systems recover far less solids than their conceptual promise suggests. citeturn6view0turn12view0turn21view0turn31search0
