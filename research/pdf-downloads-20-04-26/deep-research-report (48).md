NORSUS reports that waste analyses indicated **20–30% of the content was lost** because bags were damaged or tied poorly, and that this underperformance helped trigger the shift to separate food bins. ## What contaminated digestate can still be used for

In the legal and technical sources I reviewed, digestate/biorest is treated as a **fertiliser or soil improver**, not as a feed product. entity["organization","Mattilsynet","norwegian food safety authority"] states that compost and biorest may be used as **fertiliser or soil improvement material** if the hygienisation requirements are met, and NIBIO describes biorest as the residual product from anaerobic digestion used as a fertiliser resource in agriculture. citeturn25search1turn24search2

That means the real outlet question is not “feed versus fertiliser versus energy” in a symmetric sense. The biogas plant already extracts the **energy** part during digestion; the remaining question is whether the digestate still qualifies for **agricultural recycling**. If it meets the impurity, hygiene and product rules, it can be marketed and used as fertiliser/soil improver. If it does not, the agricultural outlet is lost even though the gas has already been produced. citeturn24search4turn25search8turn31view0

The Vestland regulatory note is unusually explicit about the consequence of failure. It says that biorest which does **not** satisfy quality requirements “will have to go to final treatment in the form of incineration” and, in some cases, landfill/deposition. The same note also points out a structural trade-off: the more aggressively a plant removes foreign material and plastic, the more food waste may be rejected with it, which can reduce biogas yield and plant revenue. citeturn31view0

That trade-off is exactly why several operators have shifted attention upstream, from post-cleaning to bag choice and source control. BIR says openly that if the only value were energy, incineration could be more rational than biogas; the point of the biogas route is to get **both** energy and a digestate clean enough to keep nutrients in food production. NORSUS’s Mobiplast report likewise notes that, in today’s Norwegian practice, plants post-treat digestate collected with **ordinary plastic bags** specifically so that the resulting biogødsel does not spread plastic into soil. citeturn20view3turn18view1

## What EU and Norwegian fertiliser law actually says

The **EU Fertilising Products Regulation 2019/1009** allows compost and digestate as component materials, but it controls plastic through the category of **macroscopic impurities**. For both compost and digestate, the relevant limit is **no more than 3 g/kg dry matter** for each of glass, metal or plastic above **2 mm**, and **no more than 5 g/kg dry matter** for the sum. From **16 July 2026**, plastic above 2 mm must be **no more than 2.5 g/kg dry matter** within that framework. citeturn15search9turn15search6

Your reference to a Norwegian “2022 fertiliser regulation” needs one correction. The binding Norwegian product regulation was still the old **2003** digestate/fertiliser rule until **31 January 2025**. Mattilsynet’s own guidance says the new Norwegian fertiliser-product regulation entered into force on **1 February 2025**, replacing the old one. Under the old rule, the total content of plastic, glass or metal pieces **>4 mm** could not exceed **0.5% of dry matter**. citeturn12view3turn16search0turn16search3

Under the **new Norwegian regulation** adopted in January 2025, the sum of glass, metal and plastic **>2 mm** in the finished fertiliser must be **below 5 g/kg dry matter**, and for products produced after **1 January 2026** the plastic fraction must be **below 2.5 g/kg dry matter**. The regulation also says operators must take measures to remove glass, metal and plastic above 2 mm **before treatment or storage**. In other words, Norway has now moved much closer to the EU logic, with a tighter size threshold and a dedicated plastic cap from 2026. citeturn15search1turn15search4turn16search14

What neither regime gives you is a dedicated, numeric compliance limit for **microplastics below 2 mm**. That is an inference from the legal text itself: the thresholds are all written for particles **above 2 mm**, or in the old Norwegian rule **above 4 mm**. So the core legal problem is not that plastic is entirely unregulated; it is that the regulations are still strongest for **visible plastic**, while analytical monitoring and legal limits for smaller particles remain underdeveloped. citeturn15search9turn15search1turn16search0

## What municipalities learned when they changed collection systems

At the national level, NORSUS’s 2025 PLASMA report does **not** support a simple “paper always sorts better” conclusion. Across 50 Norwegian municipal or inter-municipal systems with kerbside food-waste collection, the average collected amount was **55.7 kg/person/year** for **paper**, **54.8 kg/person/year** for **biodegradable bags**, and **47.3 kg/person/year** for **plastic bags**. But paper also had the **highest** average share of food waste still found in residual waste (**28.0%**, versus **25.8%** for biodegradable and **25.2%** for plastic) and the highest average share of food waste not source-separated at all (**38.0%**, versus **32.5%** and **35.1%**). NORSUS therefore concludes that there is **no consistent sorting difference by bag type alone**. citeturn22view0turn22view1

ROAF is the clearest Norwegian example of a documented shift away from the classic green-bag optical system. After introducing separate food bins in pilot municipalities, the share of food waste in residual waste fell from **25.9%** in autumn 2022 to **22.3%** in autumn 2024, while the food-waste source-separation rate rose from **54.1%** to **69.4%**. ROAF links the change to the decision to scrap the one-bin green-bag system, which had suffered from the estimated **20–30% loss** caused by broken or badly tied bags. citeturn20view0turn45view0

entity["organization","Renovasjon i Grenland","waste company telemark norway"] gives a similar result. After moving from coloured-bag optical sorting to a bin-based system, the food share in residual waste fell from **37.3%** in 2021 to **31.7%** in 2023, and the food-waste return rate rose from **54.5%** to **61.3%**. The report also notes that the quality of sorted plastic improved. That strengthens the broader conclusion that **separate containers outperform shared-bin optical bag systems** when the goal is high capture and cleaner streams. citeturn45view0

entity["city","Fredrikstad","norway"] is the most useful Norwegian paper-bag trial. Before full rollout, the municipality tested **two paper bags with more than 1000 households** and received extensive negative feedback, mainly because the bags broke down. The reported average satisfaction score was only **28 out of 100**, and the municipality concluded that many residents stopped sorting because of bag failure. Fredrikstad therefore chose **biobags plus a separate bin** for rollout. Its 2024 picking analyses found **30%** food waste in residual waste in areas with food-waste sorting, compared with **42%** in areas without it. citeturn22view3turn20view1turn20view2

entity["organization","HRA","waste company hadeland norway"] and BIR are important because they show why paper is still attractive even when collection performance is not automatically better. HRA has used paper bags since 1994 and says the reason is straightforward: it does not want plastic in digestate used by local farmers. BIR says the same thing, and frames clean digestate as the real value driver of food-waste biogas. Neither source gives a before/after bag-switch measurement, because both systems are essentially “paper from the start” examples rather than conversions from long-established plastic systems. citeturn20view2turn20view3

For Fosen/Ecopro, the switch was to **certified biodegradable bags** rather than paper, coordinated across multiple Mid-Norway suppliers in 2023. Ecopro said the change was partly a response to the tightening fertiliser rules, and Fosen explains to residents that the bag material matters because fragments can otherwise follow the process into fertiliser. Again, I found no public before/after microplastic concentration series for the finished digestate. citeturn23search0turn32search0

A final point is worth stating plainly: **fully bag-free household collection is still not used in Norway**, according to Mobiplast, even though the report says it is common in parts of Europe and models it as the lowest-cost solution overall if extra bin wear and washing are manageable. For Norwegian municipal practice, the realistic comparison today is therefore **plastic/biobag versus paper versus separate-bin design**, not an already mainstream bag-free system. citeturn18view1

## Which process technologies could reduce the problem

The first point is comparative, not exotic: **mechanical post-treatment plus better source control is the most mature Nordic route today**. Lindum’s Plastfri results show that simple unit operations such as sieving, pressing, drying and separation can already remove most of the *visible* plastic in both liquid and fibrous digestate fractions. That is much closer to deployment than the more advanced options below. citeturn29view0

**Thermal hydrolysis / broader hydrothermal treatment** is technically promising but should be split into two categories. As a **sludge pretreatment** before anaerobic digestion, thermal hydrolysis is already commercially established in wastewater systems. As a **digestate decontamination step specifically for microplastics**, it is much less mature. A 2024 ACS study reported that hydrothermal treatment could remove **about 90% of quantifiable microplastics from digestate**, which is a strong laboratory signal. Recent digestate-management literature also places advanced contaminant-control treatment costs in the broad range of **€60–80 per tonne of wet feedstock**, with investment needs around **€351 per tonne of annual wet-feedstock capacity**. My reading is that this puts hydrothermal removal at **pilot to early-commercial maturity for digestate clean-up**, but with materially higher capital and energy intensity than source control or conventional post-sieving. citeturn39search0turn42search0

**Supercritical water oxidation or gasification** is a different proposition. Technically it can destroy wet organic slurries and the embedded plastic rather than merely separating them. The current real-world evidence base is strongest for **biosolids/sludge**, not food-waste digestate: the 374Water/Orange County demonstration is exactly that kind of application. Cost is the main brake. Recent cost work cited in the PFAS/biosolids context gives **€1,160–€3,380 per dry tonne** treated, and demonstration material shows system capital costs scaling into the **tens of millions of dollars** even at modest dry-tonne-per-day sizes. That makes supercritical water a **demonstration to early-commercial destruction technology**, not a near-term, low-cost Nordic solution for ordinary municipal food-waste digestate. It is most relevant where the contamination problem is so severe that the agricultural outlet is already lost. citeturn42search6turn42search10turn42search18

**Enzymatic degradation** is the least mature of the three for this particular use case. The closest commercial progress is in **PET** depolymerisation, not in the mixed PE/PP/LDPE contamination that dominates household food-waste streams and bag fragments. Recent reviews say enzymatic recycling is highly promising for contaminated or coloured PET waste and that better protein engineering could materially improve cost-competitiveness; 2025 research reporting cites **65% energy savings** and **74% cost reductions** for contaminated PET streams and targets industrial pilots at **50,000-ton** scale. But that is **not** the same as mixed-digestate cleanup. For Norwegian food-waste digestate, I would rate enzymatic destruction of plastics as **low maturity** and better seen as a long-run option for selected polymers rather than a practical near-term solution. citeturn41search2turn42search19turn41search10

The practical ranking for Nordic municipalities is therefore fairly conservative. **Most mature and cheapest:** separate food bins, non-plastic collection media where feasible, better pre-treatment, and mechanical digestate cleaning. **Potentially powerful but expensive:** hydrothermal treatment. **High-destruction niche option but very capital-intensive:** supercritical water. **Scientifically exciting but not yet fit for mixed municipal digestate:** enzymatic degradation. citeturn22view1turn29view0turn42search0turn42search10
