# Commercialised replacements for soy protein and fishmeal in Nordic aquafeeds in 2025 and 2026

## Verdict

Using a strict industrial-use standard, the ingredients that are **clearly commercialised in feeds sold to Nordic farmers** are: **microalgal oil**, **fish-processing by-products and trimmings**, and **insect meal**. The strongest hard proof comes from Skretting Norway’s 2024 ingredient disclosure, which publishes average inclusion levels per kilogram of fish feed and explicitly states that insect meal, krill meal and calanus were used in “small, but commercial quantities”. In 2024, Skretting Norway’s average feed included **0.012% insect meal**, **0.598% microalgae oil**, **3.4% fish meal from trimmings**, **5.2% fish oil from trimmings**, and **1.1% fish oil from farmed fish** in customer-specific feeds; overall “novel raw materials” averaged **0.8%** of the recipe. citeturn40view0turn39view0

For the other large producers, the evidence is more mixed. Cargill/EWOS officially states that it uses insect meal and algal oil in Norwegian salmon feeds, and that feed supplied to Lerøy in 2023 continued to include both insect proteins and algal oil. Mowi Feed officially states that **4%** of its 2024 feed composition consisted of “emerging feed raw materials”, but the public examples attached to that claim are **algal oils and pea protein concentrate**, not insect or single-cell protein. BioMar clearly proves commercial use of **microalgal oil** in salmon feed, but its 2024 public materials still describe insect meal in salmonid feeds mainly as being in validation or selective commercialisation, rather than giving Nordic recipe-level proof comparable to Skretting’s Norway disclosure. citeturn31view0turn20search0turn19search2turn17search1turn41search1turn21search0

By contrast, I did **not** find equally strong 2025/2026 evidence that **mycoprotein/single-cell protein**, **mussel meal**, or **kelp/seaweed meals** are already in mainstream commercial salmonid feed recipes sold in the Nordics by the named producers. Those categories are active in R&D, pilots, validation and pre-commercial scale-up, but the reviewed sources stop short of showing routine inclusion in Nordic sold feed formulas. citeturn22search3turn48search1turn48search3turn51search12turn51search7

## Insect protein

The most robust Nordic commercial proof is again Skretting Norway. Its 2024 disclosure shows **0.012% insect meal** in the average 1 kg fish-feed recipe, up from **0.004%** in 2023, and the company states that insect meal was used in “small, but commercial quantities”. The disclosed 2024 origin for the insect meal was **France and the Netherlands**, not the Nordics. This is therefore real industrial use in Nordic feed, but not with a Nordic insect supplier in the public data. citeturn40view0

Cargill/EWOS also clears the commercial-use threshold. In its 2023 aqua nutrition report, Cargill says it is already using “alternative ingredients like insect meal and algae oil”, and specifically says its partnership with entity["company","Innovafeed","insect ingredients france"] allows it to add insect meal to salmon feeds. In its 2024 aqua impact report, Cargill adds that feed supplied to Lerøy in 2023 “continued to include insect proteins and algal oil”. That is enough to say insect protein is commercially present in Nordic salmon feed sold by Cargill, although Cargill does **not** publish the recipe percentage. citeturn26view0turn27view0turn31view0

For Mowi Feed, the reviewed official sources do **not** prove current insect inclusion in mainstream Nordic feeds. Mowi’s 2025 Emerging Feed Raw Materials Policy lists insect meal among the categories it wants to develop, but the same public policy says that the **4%** emerging-raw-material share in 2024 feed composition “includes algal oils and pea protein concentrate”. Older Mowi policy snippets for 2022 and 2023 refer to algal oils, krill meal and pea protein, but again do not publicly confirm insect meal in the commercial recipe mix. My conclusion is therefore: **Mowi has an insect strategy, but no public 2025/2026 proof of routine insect inclusion in commercial Nordic feed recipes**. citeturn17search1turn20search0turn19search2turn18search1

For BioMar, the public picture is nuanced. BioMar’s October 2024 technical article says insect meals are moving toward larger-scale commercial implementation, but still describes “larger, full commercial scale validation trials” at customer sites. ## Single-cell protein and mycoprotein

This is the category with the largest gap between technical readiness and public proof of Nordic commercial use. Skretting’s 2023 Impact Report says that, at group level, it has “successfully used” algal oils, insects and **single-cell proteins** in commercial feed. But its detailed Norway recipe disclosures for 2023 and 2024 do **not** show any named SCP/mycoprotein ingredient in the average Norwegian recipe; the published “novel raw materials” are insect meal, krill meal, calanus, crab meal and microalgae oil. On a strict Nordic-commercial-recipe basis, that means I cannot confirm public Norway-level SCP inclusion despite Skretting’s broader portfolio statement. citeturn32search1turn40view0turn39view0

BioMar’s own 2024 technical materials place single-cell proteins squarely in the **research and validation** bucket rather than in clearly commercialised Nordic feed. BioMar says single-cell proteins, including bacterial meals, fungi and yeast, are being studied by its innovation team; it reports good trial data, including **20% inclusion** for some yeast protein in testing, but that is still not proof of inclusion in commercial feed sold to Nordic farmers. citeturn22search3turn22search5

Mowi’s policies likewise show intent rather than proof. Mowi names single-cell proteins among the emerging raw materials it is evaluating, but the public breakdown of commercial inclusion in 2022–2024 points to algal oils, pea protein concentrate and krill meal instead. I therefore do **not** count SCP as commercially deployed in Mowi’s Nordic recipes based on public evidence through 2025/2026. citeturn18search1turn19search2turn20search0

On Nordic suppliers, Finland’s Enifer is the most important name. Enifer says its first factory in Finland is fully funded, sized for **3,000 tonnes per year**, and on track for start-up in 2026; it also filed for Novel Food approval in 2024 and describes PEKILO®Aqua as an aquafeed ingredient. That is significant industrial scale-up, but it is still **new capacity coming online**, not publicly evidenced as already included in commercial Nordic salmonid feeds from the named producers. Norway also has a planned mycoprotein route through entity["company","BIO3","mycoprotein norway"] using Enifer technology, but that too is a development project, not public proof of current commercial inclusion. citeturn48search1turn48search6turn48search4turn48search3turn48search11

The state of play is best described as **late pilot / early industrialisation**, not yet transparently commercial at recipe level. ## Microalgae, mussels and marine biomass

Microalgal oil is the most clearly commercialised “novel” ingredient after fish-processing side streams. Skretting Norway’s 2024 recipe disclosure shows **0.598% microalgae oil** in the average feed formula, with origins in the **USA, Brazil and China**. Cargill says it committed in early 2022 to include algal oil in **all Norwegian feeds**, and repeated that position in its 2024 report. Mowi says its 2024 feed composition contained **4% emerging feed raw materials**, explicitly including algal oils, though without disclosing the algal share. BioMar says it was the first aquafeed company to introduce microalgae with omega-3 into commercial salmon feed in 2016 and that by October 2024 it had sold **more than 4 million tonnes of feed containing algal oil**. citeturn40view0turn27view0turn31view0turn20search0turn17search2turn21search0turn41search1

The important caveat is supply geography. In the public evidence I reviewed, the commercial algal oils used in Nordic aquafeeds are **not produced in the Nordics**. Skretting Norway’s 2024 origin disclosure points outside the region, and BioMar’s own case story links its pioneering microalgae ingredient to fermentation on sugarcane by-products rather than to Nordic algal-oil manufacturing. So the ingredient is definitely commercialised in Nordic feed, but **not with a documented Nordic production base** in the sources reviewed. citeturn40view0turn21search0

For mussel or blue-mussel meal, I did **not** find evidence of commercial inclusion in Nordic feeds sold by the named producers. The clearest contemporary signal is actually that the work is still developmental: Nofima’s project list includes “Mussel up – Blue mussel protein for aquafeeds” running from **2023 to 2026**, which strongly suggests this category has not yet crossed into routine commercial formula use. citeturn51search12

For seaweed and kelp, I likewise did not find public proof of routine inclusion in the named producers’ Nordic commercial salmonid formulas. Nordic Innovation’s 2025 material shows seaweed and mussels as growing Nordic bioeconomy segments, but not as verified mainstream aquafeed inclusions in the way that microalgal oil, insect meal and fish trimmings now are. citeturn51search2turn45search0

A small but real adjacent marine-biomass category is zooplankton. Skretting Norway’s 2024 feed disclosure includes **0.010% calanus**, harvested in Norway, again explicitly described as commercial though small volume. That means Nordic-sourced marine biomass is in commercial use, just not in the mussel/kelp forms the market often expects. citeturn40view0

## Processing side streams and by-products

This is the most deeply commercialised alternative basket in the Nordics. Skretting Norway’s 2024 published formula contains **3.4% fish meal from trimmings**, **5.2% fish oil from trimmings**, and **1.1% fish oil from farmed fish** in customer-specific feeds. Skretting further states that farmed-salmon oil is made from category 3 salmonid by-products from Norway, Iceland or the Faroe Islands and that Skretting Norway has used salmon oil in customer-specific feeds for the past decade. citeturn40view0turn35search0

Cargill/EWOS is also clear on circular inputs. In its 2023 aqua report, Cargill says **49% of coldwater feeds** were made up of co-products in 2022; fish trimmings used for oil reached **60%** and for meal **36%** in 2022; and by 2023 **34.5% of all marine ingredients** were sourced from trimmings. These are not full-recipe percentages for one Norwegian salmon diet, but they are unmistakable evidence that fish-processing side streams are already industrially embedded in Nordic coldwater feed. citeturn28view3turn28view4turn31view0

Mowi publicly reports the same direction. Its 2025 policy says it is increasing the use of fish trimmings in both integrated and externally sourced feed, and its TNFD disclosure says that in 2024 **28% of fish meal** and **29% of fish oil** used by Mowi Feed originated from trimmings. Again, those are marine-ingredient shares rather than full-formula shares, but they still confirm real industrial use. citeturn52search4turn52search11

What I could **not** verify publicly in 2025/2026 was routine commercial use, in the named Nordic producers’ salmonid feeds, of **slaughterhouse by-products** such as poultry meal, blood meal or porcine products at current recipe level. Nofima’s current project list still treats “Chicken meal for salmon” as a **2024–2027** research programme, which is a strong sign that this remains developmental rather than an openly documented mainstream Nordic commercial inclusion. Likewise, I found no public evidence that brewing side streams are already a named, routine ingredient in sold Nordic salmonid feeds from the target companies. citeturn51search7turn22search3

If one extends “side streams” beyond feed-grade use, Finnish company Hailia is relevant but important to classify correctly: Hailia upcycles fish-processing sidestreams into **human food**, not into a documented aquafeed ingredient sold to the named feed companies. So Hailia belongs in the Nordic circular-bioeconomy picture, but **not** in the short list of proven aquafeed ingredient suppliers for this question. citeturn49search14turn49search16

## Producer and supplier matrix

For **Skretting**, the answer is the clearest and most quantitative. Public Norway data prove commercial use of insect meal, microalgae oil, calanus and fish-processing by-products in feed sold in Norway. The disclosed 2024 average recipe shares are **0.012% insect meal**, **0.598% microalgae oil**, **0.010% calanus**, **3.4% fish meal from trimmings**, **5.2% fish oil from trimmings**, and **1.1% fish oil from farmed fish** in customer-specific feeds, with total novel raw materials at **0.8%**. Nordic suppliers are confirmed for calanus and salmon-oil streams, but not for insect meal or algal oil. citeturn40view0turn35search0

For **BioMar**, the ingredient I would count as firmly commercialised in Nordic aquafeeds is **microalgal oil**. BioMar’s own 2024–2025 material says it introduced microalgae with omega-3 into commercial salmon feed in 2016 and has since sold more than **4 million tonnes** of feed containing algal oil. Its public disclosures do not give a Nordic recipe percentage. Insect protein is clearly close to commercial scale and demonstrably commercial in some geographies, but I did not find equally strong public evidence of routine Nordic salmonid inclusion for 2025/2026. Single-cell proteins remain in the study and validation phase in BioMar’s public materials. citeturn21search0turn41search1turn22search5turn23search2

For **Cargill/EWOS**, I would count **insect meal**, **algal oil**, and **fish-processing by-products/trimmings** as commercialised. The company says algal oil is in all Norwegian feeds, that insect meal is being added to salmon feeds, and that feed to Lerøy in 2023 continued to include insect proteins and algal oil. Public percentage disclosure is at ingredient-basket level rather than recipe level: **34.5% of marine ingredients from trimmings in 2023**, and **49% co-products in coldwater feeds in 2022**. citeturn27view0turn31view0turn28view3

For **Mowi Feed**, I would count **microalgal oil** and **fish trimmings** as commercialised. The official aggregate figure is **4% emerging feed raw materials** in 2024, including algal oils and pea protein concentrate; trimmings represented **28% of fish meal** and **29% of fish oil** used by Mowi Feed in 2024. I would **not** count insect meal, mycoprotein or bacterial SCP as proven commercial Mowi ingredients in Nordic sold feed on currently public evidence. citeturn20search0turn52search11turn52search4

## What is commercial today and what is not

The cleanest answer is therefore this. **Commercial today in Nordic sold feeds:** microalgal oil; fish meal and fish oil from trimmings; fish oil from farmed-fish side streams; insect meal, but at very low published inclusion rates in the only fully transparent Nordic salmon-feed disclosure I found. **Not yet publicly proven as mainstream Nordic commercial feed ingredients:** mycoprotein and other single-cell proteins from Nordic producers; mussel meal; kelp/seaweed meal; and slaughterhouse or brewing side-stream proteins as named routine components in commercial salmonid recipes. citeturn40view0turn31view0turn20search0turn41search1turn48search1turn51search12turn51search7

The practical consequence is that, as of 2025/2026, **the “real” commercial alternatives in Nordic aquafeed are still dominated by three things**: omega-3 from microalgae, protein and oil from fish-processing side streams, and small but genuine use of insect meal.
