title: "Iceland’s Only Flour Mill Set to Be Scrapped"
---
Iceland’s only flour mill is expected to be dismantled after the weekend. A food security expert has called for immediate government intervention, warning that the mill—a key piece of national infrastructure—could be lost for good.

## **Government under pressure to intervene amid concerns over food security**

The country’s only flour mill is set to be scrapped after this weekend, raising alarm among food security experts. With no domestic milling operation to replace it, Iceland will become entirely dependent on imported pre-milled flour, which has a significantly shorter shelf life than whole grains.

The company operating the mill, Kornax, has been ordered to vacate its premises in Sundahöfn, a harbour area in Reykjavík, following the termination of its lease by Faxaflóahafnir, the harbour authority. The building is expected to be demolished and the site repurposed for other commercial activities, according to statements provided to the national broadcaster RÚV.

## **Years of delays and denied permits**

Rannveig Hrólfsdóttir, Quality Manager at Kornax, [told RÚV](https://www.ruv.is/frettir/innlent/2025-03-27-eina-mylla-landsins-fer-i-brotajarn-eftir-helgi-439857) that the company had attempted to relocate its operations to Grundartangi, an industrial site northwest of the capital, as early as 2020. However, the application was ultimately denied due to proximity to existing polluting industries—despite her claim that air quality readings were better at Grundartangi compared to Sundahöfn.

“We met with the Ministry of Food and Agriculture and requested an exemption from the regulation that blocked the move, but they refused,” Rannveig said. “They told us to appeal, which we absolutely don’t want to do.”

According to Rannveig, launching a new operation on the back of a legal appeal would be “a terrible way to start.” She also emphasised that the company’s production system is fully enclosed and equipped with advanced filtration, reducing concerns about emissions.

After five years of unsuccessful attempts to secure a permit, Kornax has abandoned efforts to continue domestic milling. The company will instead switch to importing pre-milled flour.

## **Critical infrastructure, says expert**

[Speaking to RÚV](https://www.ruv.is/frettir/innlent/2025-03-27-eina-mylla-landsins-fer-i-brotajarn-eftir-helgi-439857), Helgi Eyleifur Þorvaldsson, an assistant professor at the Agricultural University of Iceland and an expert on food security, described the mill as a vital part of the country’s infrastructure.

“This is a matter of national security,” Helgi stated. “It’s truly remarkable that such an important issue should hinge on the will of a single private company that still wants to operate.”

Helgi highlighted the strategic importance of maintaining domestic milling capacity, noting that unmilled grain has a significantly longer shelf life than flour. Without a functioning mill, Iceland can no longer import whole grains for storage and later processing—effectively shrinking the country’s ability to maintain robust grain reserves.

“Other countries maintain reserves for six to twelve months, even with access to overland transport. As an island in the North Atlantic, we are in a far more fragile position. It should not fall to a single company to secure the nation’s food supply,” he remarked.

Helgi criticised the government’s inaction, noting that many neighbouring countries are actively supporting private companies to bolster reserves in light of global instability.

## **Government response: a broader review underway**

[In an interview with RÚV](https://www.ruv.is/frettir/innlent/2025-03-27-eina-mylla-landsins-fer-i-brotajarn-eftir-helgi-439857), Hanna Katrín Friðriksson, Minister of Industries, said the government is conducting a broad review of all matters related to food security. While she encouraged Kornax to reapply for a permit—expressing confidence that it would be approved—she stopped short of committing to emergency measures to prevent the mill’s dismantling.

“I’ve outlined what the company can do if it wishes to continue, but I will not intervene to stop them from ceasing operations,” she said. “If they do, we’ll have to assess the situation as part of the ongoing review.”

When asked whether the government’s food security review would be completed before the mill is dismantled on April 1, the minister replied: “Most likely not. Iceland’s food security does not depend solely on this one company’s operations.”
