Degree Project in Sustainable Technology Second Cycle 30 credits Circular Food Systems A content and discourse analysis of the Swedish case AUTHOR: ERIK BOJÖ SUPERVISOR: SOFIA LINGEGÅRD EXAMINER: MICHAEL MARTIN Stockholm, Sweden , 2023 TRITA-ABE-MBT-23549 www.kth.se ii Abstract As most sectors today, the food sector is discussing how it can contribute towards a more sustainable society. One model that has received increased attention over the last decade is the circular economy (CE). It has been proposed as an alternative to our current linear economy where waste ends up in landfills or is incinerated and is proposed as a framework that can be followed to contribute to a sustainable development within many sectors, the food sector being one of them. The aim of this study was to identify the current discourse regarding the CE at a micro level in the Swedish food sector, throughout the entire value chain, as well as map the trajectories outlined for circularity in the sector. This was done through a content analysis of Swedish magazines and media articles discussing the subject over the last five years. The study found stakeholders from the entire value chain working with initiatives related to the CE with processing companies being the most common stakeholder group. The actors mainly propose or implement ideas based on the waste hierarchy with reuse and recycling of food waste and packaging being the most common initiatives. The food sector expects circularity to lead to reduced emissions, increased self sufficiency, economic gains, and increased sustainability in general. The findings led to four narratives being formed: Circular economy as a way to combat food waste, Circular economy as a way to combat plastic packaging waste, Circular economy as a business opportunity, and Circular Economy as a net good for sustainability issues. iii Sammanfattning Liksom i de flesta sektorer idag diskuterar aktörer inom livsmedelssektorn hur de kan bidra till ett mer hållbart samhälle. Introduction Growing global concerns regarding the future of our planet has increased the focus on sustainable development measures to combat issues such as climate change, biodiversity loss, inequality, and poverty. The last decades have seen continued increases in greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions as well as material and resource extraction (Ritchie et al., 2020; Material Flows, 2021). The increase in GHG emissions is contributing to climate change with catastrophic consequences such as rising sea levels, extreme droughts and heatwaves, melted ice caps, and biodiversity loss (IPCC, 2022). The unsustainable demand for finite resources, fueled by both population and economic growth, is often illustrated by Earth Overshoot day, the date when humanity has used all the biological resources that Earth regenerates during the entire year (Overshoot day, 2023). In light of these issues, many theories, tools, models, and concepts have been proposed as ways of decreasing our environmental impact and material footprint while increasing social and economic well being. One of these is the circular economy (CE) which is being championed by, among others, the EU (European Commission, 2020), NGO:s (Ellen Macarthur Foundation, 2023a) and large corporations (Coca Cola 2018; IKEA, 2022; H&M, 2023,) as the sustainable alternative to our current linear, take-make- waste, economy. Although there is no universally agreed upon definition of CE (Kirchherr et al., 2017), the main idea is to close the loop for the resources used in a product or service and thus slow down the material flow. One of the most cited articles on the subject define CE as a “regenerative system in which resource input and waste, emission, and energy leakage are minimised by slowing, closing, and narrowing material and energy loops. This can be achieved through long-lasting design, maintenance, repair, reuse, remanufacturing, refurbishing, and recycling” (Geissdoerfer et al., 2018). The consequences of this are, according to proponents of the CE, reduced emissions and extraction of virgin materials (European Environment Agency, 2020; WRI, 2021). Another research article that has been widely cited claims that CE has the “potential to understand and implement radically new patterns and help society reach increased sustainability and wellbeing at low or no material, energy and environmental costs” (Ghisellini et al., 2016). The same article goes on to state that the ultimate goal of CE is to decouple the environmental pressure from economic growth. CE is often said to be implementable at three levels: micro (company or consumer level), meso (eco industrial parks) and macro (nations, regions, provinces and cities) (Ghisellini et al., 2016). The CE concept is being increasingly scrutinized by the scientific community (Korhonen et al., 2018; Millar et al., 2019; Corvellec et al., 2022). One of the main criticisms concerns the lack of a common definition of the concept (Kirchherr et al., 2017). Studies have also shown how the CE often fails to account for all three pillars of sustainability, with the social being especially neglected (Kirchherr et al., 2017). Because of this, some scientists argue that CE cannot be viewed as a model that contributes to global sustainable development (Blum et al., 2020). Furthermore, critique of whether the proposed implementation of CE measures 1 actually reduces environmental impacts due to rebound effects have also arisen (Zink & Geyer, 2017). However, the criticism mentioned in research articles does not seem to translate to the public discourse of CE. When governments, NGO:s and companies talk about implementing CE, the topic of conversation tends to be about reducing environmental impacts and increasing economic growth simultaneously (Ellen Macarthur Foundation, 2023b; European Commission, 2020; Svenskt Näringsliv, 2023). One sector in which the idea of CE is discussed is the food sector (European Commission, 2020; Ellen Macarthur Foundation & Material Economics, 2021; Esposito et al., 2020; Jurgilevich et al., 2016). According to the World Economic Forum (2022) the global food sector accounts for around 12% of GDP and over 40% of all jobs. The food system refers to all the processes and infrastructure that is necessary to provide food for people, and all actors that work within it. It comprises the primary production of crops, livestock, fish and other agricultural commodities, transportation, processing, packaging, retailing, wholesaling, consumption, and waste management (International Food Policy Research Institute, 2022). The food system is not an isolated system but is instead intertwined with other systems such as the climate system, ecosystem, transport system, energy system etc. Changes in any of these systems will have an impact on the food system (HLPE, 2014). The Swedish food sector is the country’s third biggest sector and had a turnover of 200 billion SEK in 2021 while employing 55 000 people (Livsmedelsföretagen, 2022a). Compared to the global average, the environmental impacts are lower. In terms of environmental impacts, the sector emits roughly 7 Mt CO2-e annually, i.e. 13% of Sweden's total emissions, and requires 7% of the country's land. Furthermore, agricultural practices use 3% of the total water use in Sweden (Jordbruksverket, 2022a; SCB, 2022; Jordbruksverket, 2022b). However, being better than the global average is rarely enough when it comes to sustainability issues. It is therefore not surprising that CE has become an element of the discourse in Sweden when discussing how the country's food sector can contribute to a sustainable development. In 2021, the government published “Circular Economy - An action plan for the Swedish transition” where CE strategies in the food sector were discussed (Klimat- och näringslivsdepartementet, 2021). In September 2022, a new government was voted into power. The action plan made by the previous government was revised and a less detailed action plan has been uploaded by the new government. This action plan does however state that a more detailed version will be published in the future (Klimat- och näringslivsdepartementet, 2023). To date there is no compilation of the different CE strategies being proposed by the various stakeholders at the micro level within the Swedish food sector. By understanding which targets and goals have been set by the stakeholders, one understands which aspects of CE they underline, which parts of the food supply chain they believe shows the most promise for CE, and which aspects of sustainability, i.e environmental, social, or economic, are emphasized. Furthermore, it could potentially highlight conflicting goals and areas of focus. Conflicting goals could be an indicator that the idea of CE is not interpreted in the same way across the value chain and that the stakeholders are not pulling in the same direction. 2 Understanding whether stakeholders share the same goals and ideas, in this report referred to as narratives, when it comes to implementing this novel concept can lay the foundation for the discussion of where the sector is heading in terms of circularity. 3 1.1 Aim and research questions The aim of the study is to identify the current discourse regarding the CE at the micro level in the Swedish food sector, throughout the entire value chain, as well as map the trajectories outlined for circularity in the sector. To reach the aim, the following four research questions were proposed: 1) Which are the main stakeholders discussing CE initiatives in the Swedish food sector and what initiatives are being proposed or have been implemented by them? 2) What are the expected positive sustainability benefits that the suggested or implemented initiatives will lead to, according to the stakeholders? 3) Do the perspectives between the different stakeholder groups differ in any way? If so, how? 4) Based on the different proposed or implemented initiatives and the expected impacts of them, what different narratives, i.e. tendencies or patterns, regarding circularity can be identified? 4 2. Background The background section is divided into three sections: 2.1 Sustainability issues in the global food sector, 2.2 Swedish Food sector and associated sustainability issues, and 2.3 Circular Economy in the food sector: a research perspective. 2.1 Sustainability issues in the global food sector Today's food system faces many sustainability challenges. Among those of highest concern are ensuring food security and nutrition for a population that keeps growing as well as supporting the livelihoods of all people working within the system, while simultaneously doing it in an environmentally sustainable way (OECD, 2023). Because these concerns are currently not being met it is a widely held opinion that our current food system is unsustainable (Chiaraluce et al., 2021). Furthermore, the demographic change, growing population, and increased economic prosperity coupled with the increased frequency of extreme weather events due to climate change will likely exacerbate the issues (Reisch et al., 2013; University of Oxford, 2023). Currently there are around 800 million people who do not have access to enough food, around two billion people who lack the most important micronutrients, and another two billion people who are overweight (University of Oxford, 2023). The main environmental impacts that the current food system leads to are climate change, eutrophication of water bodies, water scarcity, water pollution, soil degradation, and loss of habitats and biodiversity (Reisch et al., 2013). GHG emissions occur throughout the entire supply chain: using synthetic pesticides and mineral fertilizers in farming, livestock farming that emit methane and nitrous-oxide emissions, transportation, food packaging, processing, cooling and preparation of food, and waste management (Reisch et al., 2013). Annual GHG emissions from the food sector are estimated to be around 25% of total global emissions (Ritchie, 2019). Loss of habitat and biodiversity are a result of the extensive land use change that occurs in farming practices: agriculture requires around 50% of the world’s habitable land (Ritchie & Roser, 2022). This is detrimental to local ecosystems and biodiversity. Around 70% of global freshwater withdrawals are used for agriculture causing both water scarcity and water pollution (Ritchie & Roser, 2022; Reisch et al., 2013). Eutrophication is caused by the overuse of fertilizers that eventually run off into nearby bodies of water (Jordbruksverket, 2020). Furthermore, the excessive use of antibiotics risks leading to antibiotic resistance problems in the future, which is also of global concern (Reisch et al., 2013). It is estimated that a third of all food that is produced globally goes to waste. This means that all the energy, material resources, and money that went into creating this food have been in vain. The later in the value chain food goes to waste the worse since more resources have been used. Additionally, it is generally the case that the more developed a country is, the later in the value chain the food is wasted (SLU, 2021). The reasons for food waste are many: poor 5 harvesting practices, faulty storage and packaging facilities, ineffective transportation infrastructure, plate waste from meals consumed in restaurants or in the home, and restaurants over-ordering (OECD, 2021; Gustavsson et al., 2011). Contributing to a more sustainable food sector would positively impact many of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDG). The main one is SDG 2, zero hunger, but it can also be linked to reducing poverty (SDG1), health for all (SDG3), economic growth (SDG8), industry, innovation and infrastructure (SDG9), inequality (SDG10), sustainable production and consumption (SDG12), climate change (SDG13), oceans and seas (SDG14) and ecosystems, biodiversity and forests (SDG15) (University of Oxford, 2022). In 2015 the European Union (EU) adopted its first CE Action Plan: Closing the loop - An EU action plan for the Circular Economy (European Commission, 2015). It states that a CE is in line with the main EU agenda which is concerned with, to name a few aspects, economic growth, job creation, climate and energy issues and industrial innovation. The action plan identifies five “Priority Areas”: plastics, food waste, critical raw materials, construction and demolition, and biomass and bio-based products. The food system can be said to comprise all these prioritized areas except for construction and demolition, albeit to a varying degree (European Commission, 2015). Five years later, in 2020, a new action plan for the CE was presented called A new Circular Economy Action Plan For a cleaner and more competitive Europe. However, the content concerning the food system was similar (European Commission, 2020). In December of 2019 the EU passed the European Green Deal, a series of policy initiatives with the primary objective of achieving climate neutrality for the EU by 2050. At the heart of this deal was the Farm to Fork strategy, a strategy with the goal of making European food the global standard for sustainability. It addresses the entire food chain: sustainable food production, processing and distribution, consumption, and loss and waste prevention. Noteworthy is that there is no section dedicated to the CE:s role in the transition to a more sustainable food system and the concept of circularity is only mentioned explicitly five times in the text, and in those cases with little substance. The strategy does however mention the CE related strategies that were discussed in the previous action plans, i.e. waste management according to the waste hierarchy, nutrient and water recycling/recovery, and reduction and recycling of packaging (European Union, 2020). For a more detailed description of the EU strategy, see Appendix A. 2.2 Swedish Food sector and associated sustainability issues In Sweden, the agricultural industry functions within a Nordic climate, which allows for a restricted variety of crops while supporting a substantial dairy sector. The primary sector contributes to less than 1% of Sweden's overall GDP. The dairy and bovine meat sectors account for one-third of the agricultural sector's production value, while crop production contributes to around 40%, and pig, poultry, and horticulture make up the remaining 30%. Moreover, Sweden is characterized by extensive transportation distances and thinly populated regions, with roughly 60% of the population residing in rural and intermediate areas. As 6 mentioned in the introduction, agriculture uses less than 10% of Sweden's total land area (European Commission, 2023a). The country's farmers deliver around 70% of their production to the processing industry for further processing and therefore these companies are often located close to the farmers (Livsmedelsföretagen, 2022a). In total, there are about 2 800 food-producing companies in Sweden, and it is the country's third-largest industry in terms of turnover, number of companies, and production value. The dominant sectors in terms of size among Swedish food companies are slaughter and charcuterie, dairy, and bakery, which together account for almost half of the total number of employees (Livsmedelsföretagen, 2022b). The industry is exposed to tough price competition from companies in countries with lower cost levels. Within the EU alone, there are over 300,000 food producers, all of whom have access to the Swedish market. The industry is also under pressure domestically. Sweden's 2 800 food producers compete to get their products on the shelves of the three largest grocery store chains, ICA, Coop, and Axfood, who together control over 90% of the market. This makes the Swedish grocery market the most concentrated in the EU (Livsmedelsföretagen, 2022b). Price is still the clear dominant "value" that governs the business models of the food industry. Maintaining low material and food prices is one of the main reasons why resource waste is so significant (IVA, 2016). In 2021, 0.91 million tonnes of food waste, 86 kg per capita, arose in the sector. Despite the strong price focus, several other values than price have managed to establish themselves in the Swedish food market in the last 10-15 years, such as environmental and climate responsibility, health aspects, and animal rights, which have resulted in a diversity of certifications in today's food offering. Several of these new "value products" have made progress in recent years, but still account for a relatively small part of the total market. Another environmental issue that the food sector, namely the primary production sector, contributes to is the eutrophication of the Baltic Sea through overuse of fertilizers. It is estimated that 39% of the total nitrogen emissions and 45% of the total phosphor emissions from Swedish activities comes from the country's primary production (Jordbruksverket, 2020). Despite these environmental issues, Sweden can be considered a frontrunner in other sustainability aspects. For example, Sweden has the world's strictest animal welfare legislation (Livsmedelsföretagen 2022c). Sweden also has a very high standard for food safety, as both producers and authorities conduct regular checks and follow-ups on food production. The increasing antibiotic resistance mentioned in 2.1 is a very serious problem in many parts of the world, but not in Sweden, according to Livsmedelföretagen (2022d) since Swedish farmers use the least antibiotics in the EU. The crops grown by Swedish farmers on their fields are among the cleanest in the EU due to the strict rules for pesticide use (Livsmedelsföretagen, 2022d). To further accelerate the work towards sustainable food production, large parts of the Swedish food industry have committed to a sustainability manifesto linked to renewable energy in production and fossil-free fuels for transportation, reduced food waste in primary production, trade and consumer stages, recyclability of packaging, and more efficient water use (Livsmedelsföretagen 2022c). 2.3 Circular Economy in the food sector: a research perspective The implementation of a CE is believed by many to be an effective approach to mitigate the sustainability impacts caused by the agri-food sector. This strategy involves devising measures and solutions to reintegrate waste materials and by-products back into the production cycle (Chiaraluce et al., 2021). Through their review of currents trends on research on the topic of CE in the food sector, Chiaraluce et al. (2021) found that the most important areas of research are related to climate change, bioenergy/anaerobic digestion, agricultural waste, fertilizer, phosphorus, manure, and the recovery of valuable components such as antioxidants and polyphenols. In their systematic literature review of the CE and the food sector, Zhang et al. (2022) found that the main theme in the current literature for CE measures in the food sector is related to food waste management, with other popular themes being related to packaging, policies and legislation, and resource nexus. Jurgilevich et al. (2016), found that CE in the food sector concerns reducing the amount of waste, reusing food, application of by-products and food waste, recycling of nutrients, and diet changes. They go on to say that there are three stages at which these measures can be implemented: production, consumption, and waste management, and that CE measures should be implemented at all of these stages. The literature review by Chiaraluce et al. (2021) showed that research on CE in the food sector is currently lacking publications concerning 8 economic feasibility of projects, as well as the consumer perspective/role in the transition. Esposito et al. (2020) argue that “ …due to the complexity of the agri-food supply chain, it is almost utopian to define a unique circular economy model for the whole sector”. As mentioned in section 2.1, waste management is one of the most important issues facing today's food systems. Minimizing food waste and surplus1 would lead to a more circular system since it decreases the total matter consumption and thus reduces the flow of matter, which is one of the main goals of CE. The main way to reduce food surplus is through prevention. Here, technological solutions play a big part, especially on the production side. If this is not possible, reuse of the surplus food to be used for human consumption is desirable. This can be done by giving away the food to food banks or redistribution networks who will subsequently give the food to people who suffer from food poverty (Jurgilevich et al., 2016). Packaging also plays a key role when it comes to reducing food waste which is why researchers say that innovation in this field is crucial to reduce waste that occurs during transport and in retail (Chen et al., 2020). If prevention is not possible, the waste should be recycled into animal food or composting. The compost can be used as fertilizer in agriculture or for anaerobic digestion to produce biogas (Papargyropoulou et al., 2014). The food sector should strive towards cycling nutrients and closing these loops as much as possible, thus reducing the intake of virgin nutrients according to Poponi et al. (2022). Recycling of phosphor (P) is driven by two factors, the eutrophication that runoff P causes, and the scarcity of phosphate rock in nature. P can be recovered from sewage sludge from wastewater treatment plants, since humans excrete nearly all P ingested. This would increase resource-efficiency and energy-efficiency. Nutrients can also be recovered from manure which can then be reused as fertilizer (Poponi et al., 2022). Consumers have an important role to play when it comes to avoiding food waste and surplus and they need to support policies and experiments that are in line with a CE. CE solutions on a consumer level include community supported agriculture, shifting towards a more plant based diet, and minimizing food waste and surplus (Jurgilevich et al., 2016). According to Jurgilevich et al. (2016), current CE policies are often aimed at addressing food waste in the waste management sector. This end-of-pipe solution is sub-optimal and instead, policies should take a more holistic approach, addressing the concept of sustainable consumption and production across the entire value chain. They go on to propose a number of policy initiatives: there need to be incentives for producers and retailers to give away unwanted or unclaimed products, and it should not be allowed to throw away food. Households need to understand how their behavior affects the system and educational campaigns could be a way forward. Campaigns and educational programs can also address the current over-consumption of meat and instead promote more plant-based diets. Furthermore, consumers need incentives, such as packaging deposit-and-refund schemes, to 1 Food surplus is defined as “... edible, discarded food that is abandoned intentionally, has been removed from sale from e.g., a grocery store, or otherwise fails to be consumed due to various reasons ” (Jurgilevich et al., 2016) 9 choose the circular option. Policy makers should also review current subsidies and quota limits to avoid incentivising the production of crops that are not the most needed from the food production system’s point of view, are not sustainable in terms of long term land productivity, and that could be more efficiently farmed in other places. 10 3. Method The method section is divided into five sections in which each part of the project's method is described in more detail: 3.1 Literature Review, 3.2 Content analysis, 3.3 Narrative analysis, 3.4 Semi-Structured interviews, and 3.5 Method limitations. 3.1 Literature review The first step of this project was to conduct a literature review on four topics: the food industry (both the global and the Swedish), sustainability within the food industry (both global and Swedish), CE in general, and CE in the food sector. The purpose of the literature review was firstly to establish what is meant by the food industry/system and how the value chain is constructed. Secondly, it was essential to gain a broad understanding of the current sustainability issues associated with the food sector as well as where in the value chain the different issues arise. Thirdly, it gave the author insights into how the concept of CE is defined, in which sectors it is applicable in, the intentions/goals of CE, and the different actions/steps that can be taken that are in line with a CE. Fourthly, reading specifically about CE in the food sector gave a broad idea of which sustainability issues that could potentially be tackled through the implementation of CE according to current research. The information gained from the literature review was used in the introduction and background part of the report. Sources were found using Web of Science, Google Scholar, and Google, meaning both academic and gray literature were read. Search words were: Food Sector, Food System, Food Sector AND sustainability, Food System AND Sustainability, Circular economy, Circular economy AND food sector, Circular economy AND criticism, Circular economy AND sustainability, Circular economy AND EU, mat sektor Sverige, livsmedelsindustrin Sverige, hållbarhet svensk matsektorn, and hållbarhet svensk livsmedelsindustri. Finding sources for circular economy + food sector was surprisingly difficult and it seems like this is an area where more research needs to be done. This was supported by Chiaraluce et al. A content analysis is a “systematic procedure for describing the content of communications” (Merriam, 2009, p.152) and is concerned with the frequency and variety of messages pertained in the analyzed documents (Merriam, 2009, p.205). Seuring & Gold (2012) note that a content analysis should ideally be performed by multiple researchers to “ensure the validity and reliability of the results” and not be affected by the judgements and bias of a single researcher. Since this thesis is not done by multiple researchers, this can be seen as a weakness of the report. Sources for the content analysis were found using the search engine Retriever which displays various Swedish media sources such as articles from prominent newspapers and technical and trade magazines. It does not show company websites. Four searches were conducted for the period 2018.01.01 - 2023.03.15. It was assumed that the EU Circular Economy Action Plan, implemented in December 2015, had an impact on the business initiatives but that a delay could be expected in terms of amount and quality of the sources. To organize and manage the data from the sources found in Retriever, codes were assigned to words, sentences, or paragraphs of an article that answered the posed research questions using the software NVIVO version 12. According to Merriam (2009, p.173) codes are “nothing more than assigning some sort of shorthand designation to various aspects of your data so that you can easily retrieve specific pieces of the data. The designations can be single words, letters, numbers, phrases, colors, or combinations of these”. These segments, or units of data, should meet two criteria: They should be heuristic, meaning that the data should provide useful information (useful in that they help answer the research questions) and also inspire the reader to think more broadly about the topic at hand, and be the smallest piece of information that is self sufficient (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). 13 Initially an inductive approach to coding was taken, meaning new codes were created as data was being uncovered. As more data was being analyzed, the process became more deductive, i.e. previously created codes were now being matched with the new data (Merriam 2009, p. 183). After working through all the data and assigning codes to relevant information, an overview of the codes was conducted. Similar codes that were worded differently but contained the same core information were merged. The codes were then assigned to a suitable category, a process called analytical coding (Merriam 2009, p.179). Categories are “ conceptual elements that ‘cover’ or span many individual examples (or bits or units of the data you previously identified) of the category” and are a crucial part of data analysis (Merriam 2009, p.180-181). Four different main folders were created in NVIVO where categories and subcategories would subsequently be placed: “ Main stakeholders identified”, “Proposed or implemented CE actions”, “Expectations from stakeholders when implementing CE measures ” and “Perspectives of stakeholder groups”. These folders were directly related to research questions 1-3. All the created categories followed the five criterias listed by Merriam (2009, p.186-187) that constructed categories should meet. Categories should be 1) Responsive - the categories are the answers, or part of the answers, to the research questions 2) Exhaustive - all data that is relevant to the study should fit in a category or subcategory 3) Mutually exclusive - the data can only be placed in on of the categories 4) Sensitizing - The names of the categories should be understandable to a reader who is not familiar with them. 5) Conceptually congruent - the categories at the same level should possess an equal level of abstraction. For Main stakeholders, categories were created based on the food value chain: primary production, transportation, food processing, packaging, grocery stores, restaurants, consumers, and waste management. The subcategories were named after the stakeholder, and placed under the category (i.e. part of the value chain) in which it belongs. In this case, the codes and the subcategories were the same, i.e. if the packaging company Tetra Pak was mentioned in an article, it was coded as “Tetra Pak” and then moved to the subcategory “Tetra Pak”, which was put in the Packaging category. In Appendix B, a screenshot of the NVIVO structure is shown to facilitate the understanding for the reader. The categories for the folder Proposed or implemented CE actions were based on the waste hierarchy. The waste hierarchy is a framework that prioritizes the management of waste in order to reduce its environmental impact. It generally consists of the five steps according to European Commission (2022): 14 1. Prevention - This involves reducing the amount of waste generated in the first place 2. Reuse - This involves using a product or material again for its original purpose, or for a new purpose, without any significant processing. 3. Recycling - This involves converting waste into a new product, through processes such as mechanical recycling or chemical recycling. 4. Recover - When none of the above three options are available, recovering the energy contents of the product is the preferred option. 5. Disposal - This is the final option and involves the safe and environmentally sound landfilling of waste that cannot be prevented, reused, or recycled. The waste hierarchy is closely interlinked with CE and the EU views following the waste hierarchy as ”key steps to achieving a greener, more circular economy” which is why it was chosen as a framework for the categories and subcategories in this part of the content analysis (European Commission, 2023b). The codes created were descriptions of what the stakeholder stated was an example of their CE endeavors. If the same stakeholder described the same initiative, but in a different article, this was coded both of the times. After coding, suitable subcategories became evident. The prevention category required two subcategories: food waste and packaging. The reuse category required four subcategories: food to people, food to animals, by-products to new products, and packaging. Recycling required two subcategories: Nutrients from waste and packaging. Recovery energy required two subcategories: food to biogas and excess heat to production. No codes were found to match the “disposal” category and it was thus disposed of. The NVIVO structure can be viewed in the Appendix B. This is one of the levels of data analysis possible for quantitative analysis according to Merriam (2009, p.188). To answer research question 4 and identify current circularity narratives in the Swedish food sector a more abstract type of data analysis was performed. Narratives are socio-technical imaginaries that outline potential visions that the industry has for a certain topic (Jasanoff & Kim, 2015; Mutter, 2019; Lazarevic & Valve, 2017). They are stories that define a problem, elaborate on the consequences and eventually outline different solutions (Roe, 1994). They require subjective judgements about what or who is excluded and included and what issues, questions and solutions are prioritized (Franceschini & Pansera, 2015). By identifying the food sector's current narratives different trajectories/potential futures for circularity can be identified. The narratives were identified by taking a birds eye view of the categories and subcategories and crosscutting between them to identify the most prevalent themes that the stakeholders are presenting. It also entailed analyzing what stakeholders did not talk about and the assumptions or conclusions that can be made from this. An important criterion was that the themes were mentioned amongst many stakeholders across the entire value chain. The purpose of this criterion was to ensure that a message that was mentioned frequently but only by one stakeholder was not misconstrued as a sector narrative, since they are a single stakeholders narrative. This resulted in four different narratives, presented in section 5. 3.4 Semi-Structured interviews To complement the data found in the content analysis, three semi-structured interviews were conducted. Semi-structured interviews are a qualitative approach that involves asking predetermined open-ended questions using a written guide. The guide is flexible and allows for a more in-depth discussion of the topic, since the questions do not have a fixed range of responses and can be asked in any order (Given, 2008). The point of the interviews was to allow stakeholders who were prevalent in the content analysis to give deeper explanation of their circular endeavors and to clarify any potential uncertainties. Ragn-Sells, Foodhill, and Axfoundation were the stakeholders chosen to be interviewed. Ideally, stakeholders from the processing, packaging and grocery store sector would have been interviewed to cover the 16 entire value chain but due to difficulty in finding stakeholders who had time to be interviewed, these three stakeholders were chosen. Ragn-Sells (a waste management company) was contacted because they were the stakeholder found most readily in the data analysis and understanding the reasons for this was deemed interesting. Foodhill (a primary production company) was contacted because they were mentioned in many articles in 2018, in which they displayed ambitious ideas. However, only one article was found after 2018 and the interview gave Foodhill a chance to explain what has happened since these articles came out and explain the lack of articles in recent years. Axfoundation is a non-profit organization that works with very concrete circular food projects and learning more about the motivation behind this and the real world challenges they have found was expected to bring interesting insights. The interviews were held online using Microsoft Teams or Zoom and were roughly 30 minutes long. They were, after consent had been given, recorded. One recording (the one with Foodhill) was corrupted which aggravated the note taking process and the information from this interview is thus not as extensive as was intended. The interviewees were informed about the purpose of the project as well as the purpose of the interview. The questions that had been prepared beforehand, which can be viewed in Appendix D, constituted the basis for the interview. However, as the interviewee answered, new questions that had not been prepared beforehand arose and were asked. The interviews were conducted in both English and Swedish. After the interviews, the recordings were played and the most relevant pieces of information for the study were transcribed. 3.5 Method limitations As with all methods, the methods used in this report have its limitations. The main one is related to the search words used. Only four searches were conducted and there are many CE related words that could have been coupled with “mat” and “livsmedel”. Furthermore, companies who are conducting circular initiatives but do not explicitly mention “circular” or “circular economy” are explicated from the results in Retriever. This might exclude some interesting experiments or projects and could potentially change the overall picture of how circularity is implemented, although the latter does seem unlikely due to the relative convergence of ideas found. Another limitation is the number of articles that were behind paywalls. Although it was not quantified by the researcher, an estimation is that 10-20% of the articles that contained relevant information were behind paywalls. This likely meant that some stakeholders who do explicitly talk about circularity or CE were left out of the report. The use of Retriever as a search engine is another limitation of the study. Retriever does not show company websites, which means that companies that do not talk publicly about CE will not be found. For example, Paulig Group was found to talk about CE on their website, something that was discovered during the literature review (Paulig Group, 2023). However, Retriever did not find a single article where Paulig Group spoke about CE, and they are thus not found in the content analysis. As mentioned in 3.3 Semi-structured interviews, the initial 17 goal was to interview representatives from all parts of the value chain. Since this was not done, it leaves out useful information that limits the depth of the overall analysis in certain parts of the value chain. Conducting a content analysis as a single researcher can also be seen as a limitation of the study since it involved a degree of subjective interpretations. Should another researcher conduct the same experiment it seems likely that the quantitative parts of the report would differ since quotes and statements could be interpreted differently. However, the overall trend should remain the same, again due to the relative convergence of ideas found. 18 4. Recycling food waste is the notion of recovering nutrients from the waste and using it as compost or digestate from anaerobic digestion as fertilizer. Recycling packaging entails collecting the used packaging, sorting them, and then processing them in a way that creates a new product. 4.2.3.1 Nutrients from waste Figure 5 shows that recycling nutrients from waste was the most popular subcategory in the content analysis of proposed or implemented CE actions and figure 12 below shows that it was mainly the primary production and waste management sector that was concerned with this issue. Out of the 29 articles, eleven were from Ragn-Sells and their representatives that all contained more or less the same message, which is that Sweden needs to increase its recycling of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium and that Ragn-Sells has the technology to 29 do so (Hedström, 2022; Larshans & Finsson, 2020; Skog, 2018; Larshans, 2022). They have developed three different technologies: Ash2Phos (recycling of phosphor), Aqua2N (recycling of nitrogen), and Ash2Salt (recycling of potassium). Furthermore, they believe that legislation should require a minimum of 50% of the phosphorus from sewage sludge to be returned to agricultural land (Cirkulation, 2018). Ash2Phos incinerates sewage sludge while removing most of the toxins and extracting more than 90% of the phosphor from the sludge (Olofsson, 2021). The Ash2Salt technology extracts potassium from fly ash, a by-product from energy production. The first facility was inaugurated in April 2023 and the company has already signed deals with enough district heating producers that the maximum volume of fly ash that they can process has been reached6. The Aqua2N technology captures the nitrogen in wastewater treatment plants and converts it into ammonium sulfate which can be used as fertilizer in primary production (Larshans, 2022). An initiative in line with Ragn-Sells wishes is currently taking place in Stockholm. Analysis - CE narratives in the Swedish food sector Through summarizing and crosscutting the sections of chapter 4, four different narratives emerged from the content analysis: Circular economy as a way to combat food waste, Circular economy as a way to combat plastic packaging waste, Circular economy as a business opportunity, and Circular Economy as a net good for sustainability issues. These four narratives are presented in the following section. Each narrative is further explained and motivated, based on the findings of chapter 4. They are also contrasted to current research and findings from the literature review. The first two narratives describe how the food sector applies circular economic thinking as a way to combat current sustainability issues in the sector. The third and fourth narratives describe the food sector's main motivations to contribute to a CE and the associated expected positive outcomes of those contributions that the stakeholders state. The most coded initiative, recycling nutrients from waste, was not considered a food sector narrative since Ragn-Sells were responsible for around half of these codes. Furthermore, it was not something that was prevalent throughout the entire value chain. 5.3.1 Circular economy as a way to combat food waste The first narrative that was found was that the Swedish food sector views CE as a way of dealing with food waste. It was the most significant theme discovered in the content analysis, coded in 45% of the articles in 4.2. This finding is in line with the studies mentioned in section 2.3 by Zhang et al. (2022) and Jurgilevich et al. (2016) who say that the primary target of CE in the food sector is often food waste management. Examples of reducing the waste were given in primary production, processing, grocery stores, restaurants, consumers, and waste management meaning the narrative could be found throughout the value chain. However, the processing companies were the most prominent stakeholder group talking about the issue, with both established companies such as Löfbergs, Skånemejerier, and Fazer as well as startups such as Cashewmeetly, Mycorena, and Rscued identified in the content analysis. Suggested CE measures were found at all steps of the waste hierarchy, as evident in 4.2 and 4.4. However, the lion’s share of the articles are concerned with different ways of reusing the food waste: i.e. to feed people, to feed animals, or to create new food products from by- products. The ideas for creating new food products from by-products were diverse which is not surprising since most of the processing companies operate on different markets - what they did share was the overall focus on this part of the waste hierarchy. While the processing sector focused on reusing food waste, the primary production sector focused on producing renewable energy in the form of biogas. As mentioned in section 2.1 and 2.2 waste is one of the most urgent sustainability issues the food sector faces and it is thus perhaps not surprising that the Swedish food sector would view CE as a tool to combat these issues. 46 Ghisellini et al. (2016) writes about the lessons learned from successful implementation of circular activities and how they require the cooperation between multiple actors of society. This is something that many stakeholders seem to have grasped considering the amount of examples of stakeholder cooperation such as Löfbergs and Circle K, The circular coffee community, grocery stores donating food to restaurants and food banks, Greenfood and Rscued Fruits etc. that were found in the analysis. However, it is interesting to note how little attention is paid to the consumers' role in mitigating food waste considering most of the waste (69%) happens in this step, as was shown in Figure 1. This finding is similar to Esposito et al. (2020) who in their systematic literature review found that only 4.6% of the research articles looked at food waste from a consumption perspective. It is an indication that stakeholders place a system boundary for circular action around their own practice. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that the action most desirable according to the waste hierarchy, prevention, is the category with the least initiatives. Preventing food waste can therefore not be viewed as part of the narrative. According to Leipold et al. (2021), this is likely because “one cannot valorize something that has been prevented” meaning companies can make money from reusing food waste - but cannot from preventing food waste. Axfoundation also noted how creating products from by-products is popular among companies since it allows additional revenue streams12. It is therefore not surprising that the “Reuse by-product to new product” was the most popular circular intention since this creates another stream of revenue, that donating food to people or animals does not. This is problematic according to Corvellec et al. (2022) who argue that viewing a waste as a potential resource reduces the willingness of the actor to prevent the waste from occurring. The question that needs to be answered in order to determine the net environmental effect of these new products is whether or not these new products actually replace other primary production products. If they do, then the goal of CE can be said to be achieved assuming the energy and material/resources for creating the secondary product is less than creating the primary product. If they do not, and they instead simply become a second stream, the net result is that the impacts of this secondary production are added to the impacts of the primary production. This phenomenon is called CE rebound and was popularized by Zink & Geyer (2017). Answering this is beyond the scope of this report but could be subject to further research. 5.3.2 Circular economy as a way to combat plastic packaging waste The idea of using circular thinking to combat plastic packaging waste was also prominent in the literature, with around 30% of the articles in section 4.2 relaying information on how stakeholders are working on reducing their plastic packaging waste. As shown in 4.4, the main stakeholder groups concerned with this were processing, packaging, grocery stores, restaurants, and waste management, meaning it was a theme that could be seen all through 12 Interview Axfoundation representative 24 May 2023 47 the food sector value chain. The fact that so many new companies such as &Repeat, Bower, Panter, and Vytal have been created in recent years to work within this space also implies that these companies have seen a demand for their services from stakeholders within the Swedish food sector. Examples of preventing plastic packaging waste were few which is surprising considering the use of less packaging should lead to lower costs related to packaging for companies (Fedewa et al., 2023; Dublino, 2023). Instead, examples of reusing and recycling plastic packaging were more common. When it comes to reuse of packaging, many of the initiatives proposed require a change of behavior on part of the consumer. For example, consumers need to return packaging to the restaurant that they bought the food from, when they are used to throwing it in the bin at home. Although this might initially be challenging, Ghisellini et al. (2016) argue that this form of consumer participation is crucial to reach the goals of CE. Most of the initiatives related to reuse were new ones, with the Svenska Retursystem system of using boxes and pallets to transport food products being the only long lasting example of reuse of packaging. Articles about increasing the amount of plastic packaging waste that gets recycled was found to be the most frequent way of dealing with the issue. This means that the actors are struggling to combat plastic packaging waste through initiatives higher up on the waste hierarchy and that many actors are equating CE to recycling. A report from IVA (2016) found that Swedish food companies who were interviewed believed that the recycling stage was the stage that had received most attention up to that point in terms of circularity. As outlined in this report, this narrative is still very prevalent within the food sector. It is likely that the discourse surrounding recycling of plastic has been influenced by two EU initiatives: the Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) and A European Strategy for Plastics in a Circular Economy (European Commission, 2018; Naturvårdsverket, 2022). EPR is a policy approach where producers are held accountable for the collection and treatment of certain products at the end of their life cycle while the European Strategy for Plastics in a Circular Economy has set up goals stating that plastic packaging should be recycled at the same rate as packaging of other materials by 2030. Furthermore, all packaging should be designed in a way that allows for cost effective reuse or recycling. Löfbergs explicitly state that they are motivated by the European strategy for plastic (Pettersson, 2020b). Only one project was found where the goal was to improve the market for recycled plastic. The lack of a market for many recycled plastic types is relevant when discussing the goals that many stakeholders (mostly processing companies) have set to make their packaging “100% recyclable”. Although it is not specified what the criterias for making a package 100% recyclable are, it is likely that they mean recyclable from a technical, rather than economical perspective, since the companies have more control over the former. If there are no buyers on the market, the packaging will not be recycled despite it being technically feasible and the potential sustainability benefits will be lost. In other words, even if the goal of creating 100% recyclable packaging is reached, there is no guarantee that the packaging will actually be recycled. Considering how crucial it is for recycled plastic to be more affordable than virgin 48 plastic, it is concerning that not more stakeholders spoke about ways of improving the market. 5.3.3 Circular economy as a business opportunity The third narrative differs from the first two in that it does not describe how CE can combat a problem, but instead focuses on the positive outcome that is expected when implementing circular initiatives and thereby the underlying motivation for the stakeholders to pursue circularity. The narrative that CE can be seen as a business opportunity is apparent in two ways. Firstly, as mentioned in 4.3.5, a few (mostly established) actors explicitly mention how their CE ventures are expected to improve their sales. However, most of them do not specify how, why, or in what way they expect the circular ventures to lead to economic growth. Secondly, as can be seen in Figure 3 and in Appendix C, many actors (35%) that were found in the content analysis were startups/ recently started initiatives with the stated purpose of contributing to a CE within the Swedish food sector. These startups did not explicitly talk about the potential financial rewards from their business but instead preferred to talk about the different sustainability impacts that their company contributes to. However, it can implicitly be understood that these actors believe to have identified a gap in the market for their product or service, and believe there is a market for them in which money can be made. The idea that a CE will contribute to economic growth is common in the mainstream literature about the concept. Kircherr et al. (2017) found that practitioners of CE mentioned economic prosperity as the most frequent reason for undertaking circular initiatives while Ghisellini et al. (2016) state that the ultimate goal of the concept is to decouple the environmental pressures from economic growth. Outside the scientific field, the idea of decoupling and continued growth is promoted by many actors such as the EU, World Bank, Ellen Macarthur Foundation, Accenture, and McKinsey Group (World Bank 2022, Accenture, 2015; European Environment Agency, 2021; Ellen Macarthur Foundation, 2023; McKinsey, 2016). It is therefore not surprising that many of the actors in the Swedish food sector display the same expectations. Although striving for economic growth is a prerequisite for any company, some researchers (proponents of the post-growth movement) would argue that this narrative stands to undermine the fourth narrative, Circular Economy as a net good for sustainability issues. In their report Decoupling Debunked, Parrique et al. (2019) draw the conclusion that empirical evidence fails to support the notion of a decoupling of economic growth from environmental pressures. This is true for land use, energy, GHG:s, materials, water, water pollutants, and biodiversity loss. Going one step further, Parrique et al. (2019) argue that it is unlikely that this type of decoupling will happen in the future. In the few cases where decoupling was found, it has been a case of either relative, local or temporary decoupling. This finding means that if the circular initiatives lead to increased earnings for the companies and thus overall economic growth, it will also lead to increased pressures on our environment. 49 5.3.4 Circular Economy as a net good for sustainability issues The fourth and final narrative is similar to the previous one in that it is a summary of both the implicit and explicit expectations and motivations that stakeholders have when it comes to their circularity endeavors. The content analysis made it clear that stakeholders view CE as a way to reach desirable sustainability outcomes. Section 4.3 shows the explicit expectations that stakeholders have, which can be said to give examples of improvements within all three dimensions of sustainability, with a skewness towards climate issues. Almost half of the stakeholders associate circularity with reducing emissions - a rather narrow view of environmental improvement. Examples of environmental issues that are important to the food sector that were not mentioned are biodiversity, water, and eutrophication issues. As mentioned in section 4.3.1, some stakeholders had performed calculations on how much emissions had been reduced by, giving the statements credibility. However, many simply stated, rather vaguely, that their initiatives are “beneficial for the climate”. These statements should therefore be critically analyzed and not taken at face value. The narrative that CE contributes to sustainability can also be found by analyzing that which did not arise in the content analysis. Firstly, not a single article was found to be critical or skeptical of CE in the Swedish food sector. Furthermore, discussions about potential trade- offs when implementing CE were also missing, indicating that the stakeholders, at least publicly, view the CE initiatives as a win-win situation for everyone involved. Secondly, the number of articles stating their expectations of what their initiatives would lead to (4.3) were less than half as many compared to the number of articles talking about their initiatives (4.2). In the rest of the articles the outcomes in terms of sustainability benefits of the initiatives are not clear, but it is implicitly understood that they are positive, although in what sense is up to the reader to interpret. A common statement was “ By doing this, we help contribute to a CE” - with no further explanation of in what way this is positive. This could be described as a policy legend, a term which Lazarevic & Valve (2017) describe as “a particular form of taken-for-granted statement”. It shows how these actors have accepted the idea that the CE will contribute to a more sustainable society. This view was also visible in the interview with Axfoundation who stated that the exact expectations are not always clear before a circular project, however they are implicitly assumed to exist 13. Interestingly one of the fundamental points of CE and the ones that if implemented should lead to reduced environmental pressures, reduced virgin material use and increased material efficiency (Zink & Geyer, 2017), was only coded two times each - i.e. not enough times to be seen in the hierarchy chart of figure 16. The food sector seems to be more concerned with end-of-pipe solutions rather than reducing the recourse input. Furthermore the CE has, as previously mentioned, been criticized for not improving social issues to the extent that a framework that aims to contribute to sustainable development should. For example, Murray 13 Interview Axfoundation representative 24 May 2023 50 et al. (2017) state “It is unclear how the concept of the Circular Economy will lead to greater social equality, in terms of inter- and intra-generational equity, gender, racial and religious equality and other diversity, financial equality, or in terms of equality of social opportunity. “ Based on the findings of this report, the Swedish food sector does not believe that circularity is the answer to these important questions. 51 6. Discussion The discussion section is divided into two sections, 6.1 Stakeholders found involved in promoting CE in the Swedish food sector and 6.2 Proposed or implemented CE initiatives. The sections discuss and compare the main findings in the results with current literature on the topic. Since the fourth narrative described in 5.3.4 can be seen as a summary and discussion of the results from section 4.3, this part is not discussed in this section. 6.1 Stakeholders involved in promoting CE in the Swedish food sector In total, 51 stakeholders were found in the data collection. Considering there are 2800 food processing companies and almost 60 000 primary producer companies in the countries it is tempting to draw the conclusion that most stakeholders in the Swedish food sector are not pursuing CE in any way (Jordbruksverket, 2022c). However, it is important to note that most of the processing companies are small, with 1-10 employees, and most of the primary producer companies are individual companies (enskild firma in Swedish). This might limit their interest and/or ability to publicly talk about their CE endeavors. When it comes to the lack of primary producers found, a representative of Foodhill stated that primary producers often have waste streams of low value coupled with a very mixed composition. This makes the use of these waste streams to anything other than biogas or animal feed, which is already being done by most actors, very expensive and complicated which could be the explanation why they were not very prevalent in the media articles14. Processing companies are the stakeholder group that operates on the most different levels of the waste hierarchy and are overall the stakeholder group that is most active in the circular discourse. Discussing why processing companies were the most common stakeholder group lies beyond the scope of this report but could be subject to future research. However, it is interesting to note that there was no clear theme in what type of processing company (in terms of what they sell/produce) that spoke about CE. In other words there does not seem to be any specific part of the food processing sector in Sweden that is particularly active in the transition to a circular food system. To understand whether the biggest processing companies are working with circularity, a comparison between Figure 3 and the list of Sweden's 20 largest processing companies can be made. In so doing, it turns out that only two of these companies, Orkla and Fazer, were mentioned more than three times and thus are visible in the hierarchy chart. In Appendix C, where stakeholders mentioned once or twice can be found, five of the companies, Nestlé, HKScan, Coca Cola, Arvid Nordquist, and Arla can be found. This means that 13/20 of the biggest processing companies in Sweden were not found once in the literature. Although no research as to why stakeholders in the food sector specifically would shy away from the CE has been done, articles can be found that have summarized why companies in 14 Interview Foodhill representative 12 May 2023 52 general (i.e from all types of sectors) choose not to engage in the CE. Companies may feel they lack targets and indicators to properly assess circularity, knowledge of which circular options exist and the benefits of them, or in-house skills related to CE since it is rarely a part of formal education at any level (European Academies Science Advisory Council, 2015). There might also be a real or perceived lack of consumer awareness and interest as well as low virgin material prices which makes circular options less profitable. In their overview of 195 articles on the topic Galvão et al. (2018) found that the main barriers for implementing CE were related to technology, policy/regulation, economic viability, management, performance indicators, customer interest and societal interest. Interestingly, a study by Kirchherr et al. (2018) which interviewed stakeholders in the EU economy about barriers to CE, technological barriers were not ranked among the most pressing circularity barriers by the stakeholders. However, as is mentioned in the same study, this is not translatable into every sector of the economy and the case for the food sector might differ. For example, in the interview with a representative of Foodhills, technological barriers were named the number one reason for why the circular plans have taken longer than first anticipated. With the exception of 2019, for which no explanation has been found, a positive trend in terms of stakeholders engaging with the circularity concept can be found (see Figure 2). It is likely that the EU CE action plans (European Commission, 2015; European Commission, 2020) coupled with the Swedish government's action plans (Klimat- och näringslivsdepartementet, 2021; Klimat- och näringslivsdepartementet, 2023) have influenced more stakeholders in the sector to adapt or apply a more circular approach to their business. This may also be one of the reasons why so many new startups have been created over the last years with the explicit goal of contributing to circularity within certain parts of the value chain. Ragn-Sells representatives also believe that the food sector's focus on CE has been steadily increasing, due to the EU Taxonomy and ESG reporting requirements, although they still find the knowledge of actors to be on a rudimentary level 15. 6.2 Proposed or implemented CE initiatives The vast majority of the articles found could be coded according to the waste hierarchy which shows that it was a reasonable framework to use and that stakeholders view the different steps of the waste hierarchy to be contributing to the CE. Interestingly, both Ragn-Sells and Foodhill were critical of the waste hierarchy since it is a prescriptive framework 16;17. According to these actors, it is not always the case that the implementation of the higher steps of the hierarchy are beneficial to the overall sustainability. Instead, decisions should be made on a case by case basis. Ragn-Sells further state that they are not interested in contributing to circularity just for circularities sake since they do not believe it is always the choice that leads to the best outcomes in terms of sustainability. The idea of pursuing “ circularity for 15 Interview Ragn-Sells representative 4 May 2023 16 Interview Foodhill representative 12 May 2023 17 Interview Ragn-Sells representative 4 May 2023 53 circularity's sake”, i.e. with the assumption that circularity is always beneficial from a sustainability perspective, is further explored in Harris et al. (2021). The systematic literature review done by Kirchherr et al. (2017) showed that research papers, policy papers, and non peer reviewed reports most frequently depicted CE as a “combination of reduce, reuse and recycle activities, whereas it is oftentimes not highlighted that CE necessitates a systemic shift”. The results of this report would suggest that Swedish stakeholders follow this interpretation of CE, with the addition of energy recovery. The reason for systemic shifts not being mentioned is likely because this report has looked at the micro perspective (i.e companies/business) as opposed to meso and macro perspective where these views can be expected to be more prevalent. The overall focus on food waste, packaging, and nutrient recycling was anticipated based on the literature discussed in section 2.3. Many initiatives associated with CE in the academic literature, such as product as a service, repair, longer lasting products, were not mentioned by the stakeholders and therefore seem to be less relevant to the food sector in general. Stål & Corvellec (2018) and Lazarevic & Valve (2017) note that it is quite common for companies to claim to promote circularity while in reality only applying efforts to certain parts of their business.This seems to be the case for the established stakeholders of the food sector, since no example of was found where they stated intentions of rethinking their entire business model, something that is necessary if a company is serious about a circular transition according to Lüdeke-Freund et al. (2019). Instead, they gave examples of implementing circularity measures in specific parts of their operations. Changing business models is challenging for established companies (Teece, 2010) which could explain why the business models of the startups found in the literature are more all encompassing when it comes to CE measures. 54 7. Conclusion The aim of this study was to identify the current discourse regarding the CE at the micro level in the Swedish food sector, throughout the entire value chain, as well as map the trajectories outlined for circularity in the sector. The study found stakeholders from the entire value chain working with initiatives related to the CE with processing companies being found to be the most common stakeholder group. The actors mainly propose or implement ideas based on the waste hierarchy with reuse and recycling of food waste and packaging and recycling of nutrients from waste being the most common initiatives. The food sector expects circularity to lead to increased sustainability, with reduced greenhouse gas emissions, economic gains and increased self sufficiency being the main expectations. Identifying narratives is not a precise science and it is subject to subjective judgements on the part of the researcher. It is therefore possible that other researchers conducting a similar analysis, or even readers of this report, would have interpreted the data differently and therefore come to different conclusions regarding what themes and narratives exist. The findings led to four narratives being formed: Circular economy as a way to combat food waste, Circular economy as a way to combat plastic packaging waste, Circular economy as a business opportunity, and Circular Economy as a net good for sustainability issues. These narratives can be said to be summaries of how the Swedish food sector is currently interpreting and implementing the fairly new concept of CE and can hopefully facilitate the understanding between researchers and practitioners of the topic. 7.1 Limitation of the study The results of this study are mainly relevant to Swedish stakeholders within the food sector. The result is both a snapshot of how far the sector has come in terms of circularity and a premonition of where it is heading. This information is crucial for any stakeholder who wishes to understand and contribute to the transition. It is relevant for companies, who can learn more about other companies' initiatives and be inspired by them, and policymakers who can understand what incentives are necessary to help companies who are working towards circularity. Hopefully, stakeholders who read the report also understand that the CE is not a framework that guarantees sustainable development, and that circularity for circularity's sake is not desirable from a sustainability perspective. The findings can also be interesting for food sector stakeholders in other countries, especially member countries of the EU. The results found can act as inspiration, deterrents, or simply enlighten the stakeholders on how far Sweden has come with its circular implementations. The results should not be generalized to other sectors in the Swedish economy since each sector has its own conditions for circularity. The results are also relevant for researchers both within the CE and food sector field, providing valuable insights into the Swedish case. The method used in this study can be replicated in other countries which could lead to interesting comparisons between countries or be used to analyze the circular discourse within other sectors. The content analysis thus provides a stepping stone to further research. 55 7.2 Future research There are many areas that can be further explored in light of this report. One is to understand the absence of so many of the biggest processing companies. Although no explicit criticism of CE was found in the content analysis, perhaps the silence of these actors can be understood as criticism of the concept. It would also be interesting to examine consumers' attitude towards participating in the CE. For example, surveying customer willingness to buy takeaway food from a restaurant which uses reusable packaging, meaning they eventually have to return to the restaurant, could be performed. In relation to the previous discussion on rebound effects, research on if/how the new products from by-products that these companies produce actually replace other products, or if it just leads to more consumption, would be interesting. It would also be interesting to look at the meso and macro perspective of the Swedish food sector to “complete the picture” of the current discourse on circularity. This could be done using the same method outlined in this report, with the same search words, but choosing the articles related to those perspectives. Moving the other direction in terms of abstraction levels - a similar report like this but with the focus on one part of the food sector, such as the dairy or meat industry, could potentially yield some interesting insights. It would however require a more exhaustive list of search words, or a different search engine than Retriever, since this report has already shown that no part of the food sector is especially interested in CE. Understanding which ideas have influenced the stakeholders interpretation of CE could also lead to some thought provoking insights. For example, Lazarevic & Valve (2017) note that most of stakeholder perception and expectations of CE “originate from the Ellen Macarthur Foundation” and that many actors simply “spread this meme”. The Ellen Macarthur Foundation has produced several reports on the relation between the food sector and CE (Ellen Macarthur Foundation, 2021a; Ellen Macarthur Foundation, 2021b). These reports coupled with the EU:s CE Action Plan (European Commission, 2015; European Commission, 2020) and the Swedish Governments CE Action plan (Klimat- och näringslivsdepartementet, 2021; Klimat- och näringslivsdepartementet, 2023) would be good places to start an analysis of this sort. In a broader context - linking CE to the food sector specifically seems to be an area where research is currently lacking, something mentioned by several researchers (Chiaraluce et al., 2021) and also noted during the making of this report. This would make it easier to understand CE:s potential and educate stakeholders who are working with the issue. It would also display the current implementation barriers as well as facilitate the discussions regarding what part of the criticism that the CE concept has received is relevant for the sector. 56 8. The Action Plan (AP) starts off by stating “The transition to a more circular economy, where the value of products, materials and resources is maintained in the economy for as long as possible, and the generation of waste minimized, is an essential contribution to the EU's efforts to develop a sustainable, low carbon, resource efficient and competitive economy.” It goes on to say that a CE is in line with the main EU agenda which is concerned with, to name a few, growth, job creation, climate and energy issues and industrial innovation. The action plan described how production, consumption, and waste management of products and services all can play a role in increasing the circularity of our current system (European Commission, 2015). The action plan identifies five “Priority Areas”: plastics, food waste, critical raw materials, construction and demolition, and biomass and bio-based products. The food system can be said to, albeit to a varying degree, comprise all these prioritized areas except for construction and demolition. Waste management will play a pivotal role in the transition to a CE since it dictates how the EU waste hierarchy is effectively put into practice. The action plan states that the goal is to reduce landfilling and incineration of municipal and packaging waste and instead increase the reuse and recycling of these important waste streams. The action plan also made clear that the EU intends to meet the UN:s goal of reducing per capita food waste at retail and consumer level by 2030, according to the SDG:s. Suggestions to reduce household waste were awareness campaigns and economic incentives such as "pay-as-you- throw" schemes, but the action plan says that these are likely more effective on a national or local level. The EU also strives to facilitate food donation to food banks and as input in animal feed (European Commission, 2015). Plastic recycling was especially emphasized and deemed essential for the transition to a CE and more ambitious targets for recycling of plastic were requested. Innovation in plastics was also deemed necessary for two reasons: to increase the share of plastics that can be recycled , and to improve the preservation of food. Recycling of nutrients to be used as fertilizer was also noticed as an important part of CE. Consumers are said to play a crucial role in the facilitation or hindrance of CE, based on their decisions. This concerns decisions in retail, restaurants, and at home, concerning both what a consumer chooses to consume, but also how he or she deals with the associated waste. In order to make decisions in line with a circular society, they need to have access to relevant information to make an informed decision, which is where the awareness campaigns previously mentioned come into play. Lastly, reusing properly treated wastewater into agriculture practices was also mentioned (European Commission, 2015). A new action plan for the CE was presented in 2020 called A new Circular Economy Action Plan For a cleaner and more competitive Europe. However, the content that affects the food 71 system was similar. (European Commission, 2020) In December of 2019 the EU passed the European Green Deal, a series of policy initiatives with the primary objective of achieving climate neutrality for the EU by 2050. At the heart of this deal was the Farm to Fork strategy, a strategy with the goal of making European food the global standard for sustainability. It addresses the entire food chain: sustainable food production, processing and distribution, consumption, and loss and waste prevention. Noteworthy is that there is no section dedicated to the CE:s role in the transition to a more sustainable food system and the concept of circularity is only mentioned explicitly five times in the text, and in those cases with little substance.
